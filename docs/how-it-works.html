Gujarati in the room.
English on the screen.

A mandir plays recorded sermons most Sundays and broadcasts its own festival week once a year. Many people watching read English more easily than they follow spoken Gujarati. caption-bridge listens to the sermon and puts English words on the screen — either later in the week, or live while the swami is still speaking.

The big picture

Two jobs, one machine

Everything the project does is one of two jobs, and they share almost all of their parts: the same transcription service (Soniox), the same glossary of temple vocabulary, and the same rules about what a readable subtitle looks like.

~45 Sundays a year

The weekly job

A recorded sermon file goes in; subtitle files come out. Run once, checked in VLC, played on the projector or uploaded to YouTube. The workhorse.

7 days, once a year

The festival broadcast

The mandir's own live stream. Sound is captured as the swami speaks and captions go to the screen, YouTube, a subtitle file, and a Google Doc — in seconds.

Job one

The weekly job, start to finish

One command does the whole thing: ingest ./sermon-2026-08-16.mp4 --speaker "Swami Ji" --date 2026-08-16. A 90-minute sermon takes a few minutes. Here is what actually happens inside.

  1. Trim the video first

    Every timestamp is counted from second zero of the file you hand over. Trim the file to just the sermon and no offset arithmetic ever exists anywhere. The video itself is never modified — subtitles live beside it as separate files.

  2. Pull the sound out

    src/audio/extract.ts asks ffmpeg to lift just the audio track. Soniox doesn't want the pictures.

  3. Send it to Soniox

    A cloud service that listens to Gujarati, writes down what was said, and translates it to English — steered by the glossary (below). It sends back thousands of tiny word-pieces called tokens, each spoken piece stamped with the exact millisecond it was said.

    The full answer is saved beside the video as .soniox.json — like keeping the receipt. Run ingest again and it reuses the receipt: free, instant, and byte-identical. --force is the only way to pay twice.

  4. Build the subtitles

    The word factory (src/segments/build.ts) glues pieces into words and words into subtitle-sized chunks with start and end times. The whole next section is about this file — it is the heart of the project.

  5. Write it all in the notebook

    Every subtitle goes into Postgres: the Gujarati, the English, the timing, the speaker. This copy is the boss copy — everything else is regenerated from it.

  6. Hand out copies

    Two .srt files (English and Gujarati) appear beside the video — VLC finds them automatically. publish puts both on YouTube as caption tracks. play drives the projector overlay in sync with vMix playback, reading straight from the notebook.

Like you're five

You give the robot a film. It covers its eyes and just listens, writes everything down in its big notebook, then prints out little cards of words that pop up at the right moments. And it keeps the receipt, so if you ask again it doesn't do the work twice — it photocopies its notebook.

The heart · src/segments/build.ts

The word factory

Soniox doesn't send back sentences. It sends back pieces of words, and two strange facts about them shape the whole file. Both were invisible on small tests and only showed up on a real 69-minute sermon.

Fact one: tokens are puzzle pieces, and a leading space is the only clue. A piece that starts with a space begins a new word; a piece that doesn't is the middle of one. Join them with nothing in between:

અક્ષર snaps into અક્ષર — one word
The lord snaps into The lord — the marked space starts word two

Soniox even sends a bare " " as its own token. It looks empty, but it is a word boundary — throw it away and two words get welded together.

Fact two: translated pieces have broken clocks. Every English token arrives stamped start_ms: 0. Believe it and every subtitle drags itself to the very start of the video. So timing always comes from the spoken Gujarati pieces, and the English borrows it.

With those two facts respected, the factory runs five passes. The same pieces in always produce byte-identical subtitles out — no randomness anywhere.

  1. Sort and pair

    Split the stream into Gujarati runs and English runs, then pair each spoken run with the translation that follows it. Run-to-run, never piece-to-piece — the two streams have different lengths and their pieces don't correspond.

  2. Split the giants

    A long unbroken passage becomes one huge block. Too big for a screen, it is cut at the friendliest point available: a sentence end, else the longest pause, else the middle — and never, ever in the middle of a word.

  3. Group into subtitles

    Blocks stick together until a sentence ends or the speaker goes quiet for 4 seconds. A subtitle never spans two speakers, never exceeds ~138 characters, never stays up longer than 20 seconds.

  4. No blinking allowed

    Anything that would flash up for under 1.5 seconds is merged into its neighbour. This rule outranks the size rules — a merged subtitle may run a little long, but nothing unreadable ever ships.

  5. No overlaps

    Two subtitles must never be on screen at once. Overlapping ones get clamped, or merged if clamping would make one blink.

Last of all, the glossary clean-up pass runs over the finished English — because gluing two subtitles together can form a phrase neither of them contained, and "Swami" + "Bapa" is exactly one of the phrases it fixes.

The words arrive as LEGO bricks, not toys. The factory snaps bricks into words, words into little signs, throws no brick away (even the invisible ones), makes sure no sign flashes too fast to read, and never lets two signs fight over the screen.

Source of truth

The database is the boss

The .srt files are photocopies. Postgres is the original. Fix a bad translation in the database and every future copy — the files, YouTube, the projector — comes out corrected. Hand-edit an .srt instead and the next export silently overwrites your work.

Fixing a line takes four commands:

list                                          # find the service
show ./sermon.mp4 --from 40 --limit 5         # read around the problem
edit ./sermon.mp4 --cue 42 --text "The corrected line."
export ./sermon.mp4                           # regenerate the .srt files

Every edit records who changed it, when, and what it said before — nothing is ever lost. And re-ingesting a sermon that carries corrections is refused unless you pass --replace-edited, because otherwise a routine weekly re-run would quietly destroy someone's evening of careful fixes.

YouTube publishing is polite about the boss copy too. The notebook remembers the video id and the track ids, so a re-publish after a correction updates the existing track instead of adding a second "English". It also remembers a fingerprint of what was uploaded — publish again with nothing changed and it makes no request at all.

One day of YouTube quota — 10,000 units

each block = one video, both languages (800 units; a correction re-publish is 900) ≈ 12 videos/day

So a year's backlog (~45 sermons) is about a four-day run. publish --all spends 90% of the day's allowance, stops cleanly, and picks up tomorrow by itself — it keeps no state file, because the notebook already records what is on YouTube.

Like you're five

There is one big notebook and lots of photocopies. You never draw on a photocopy — you fix the notebook and print fresh copies. The notebook writes in pencil-proof ink: it remembers every fix, who made it, and what the page said before. And YouTube gives the robot pocket money every day; when it runs out, it just comes back tomorrow.

Job two

Captions while it happens

Festival week. Start is pressed on the Captions tab (or live --device ... in a terminal — the app calls the same code). Sound flows to Soniox over a websocket, and finished, translated lines flow back and go straight out. There is no queue, no reviewer, no scheduler: the moment a line's English exists, it is on screen.

Why a caption takes a few seconds

A caption cannot exist until the sentence it covers has finished being spoken, and then Soniox has to translate it. That wait is nearly all of the delay — measured on a real sermon, the median line is ready 7 seconds after its first word, about 1–2 seconds after its last.

There is exactly one knob: Settings → "How long to wait before captioning" (live.maxEndpointDelayMs, 2000 ms). It is a ceiling on how long Soniox may wait before declaring a clause finished. Lower it and captions arrive sooner but as fragments, chopped mid-thought. Everything else is already instant, so there is nothing else to shorten.

Seven seconds, on a ruler

The sentence is being spoken~5 s — nothing can exist yet

Nothing waits for anything else: no queue, no buffer, no scheduler. The wait is the sentence finishing — which is why the only lever trades speed for fragments.

And the screen keeps the previous line, dimmed, above the new one — two short captions read as the sentence they are, instead of one flashing past and being replaced. Neither line is ever edited; a new line arrives, the old one moves up.

Like you're five

The robot is an interpreter, and interpreters can't interrupt. It has to wait for the swami to finish the sentence before it can say it in English — the wait is the sentence being finished. It will never mumble a guess and then take it back: once words are on the screen, they stay exactly as they are.

Fan-out

Four places the live words go

Every finished line goes to up to four listeners at once, each with a different job:

SinkWhat it isWorth knowing
The screen /overlay — transparent page vMix keys over the video One URL for everything; ?lines=both adds Gujarati above English
YouTube live lines POSTed to the stream's caption ingestion URL Sent the instant they exist — nothing is held back
A live .srt subtitle file written into recordings/ as the service runs The recording can be captioned afterwards without paying to transcribe twice
A Google Doc a fresh doc per service, written as it happens Off by default; the readable record for anyone following along

The Doc deserves a word, because it deliberately does not mirror the screen. The screen speaks in short lines; the Doc writes in paragraphs — one entry per caption gave a seventy-minute sermon ten thousand unreadable lines. A paragraph ends when the speaker pauses for a second and a half, or after about 250 characters or twenty seconds, always running on to the end of the sentence. Each paragraph carries two timestamps, because they answer different questions:

11:43:07  (0:13:07)

ભક્તિ એ માર્ગ છે. સ્વામી બાપાએ આ ભૂમિને પસંદ કરી બંને નદીના મધ્યમાં અને અક્ષરધામ તુલ્ય મંદિર બનાવી.

Devotion is the path. Swami Bapa chose this land, in the middle of both rivers, and built a temple equal to Akshardham.

11:43:07 — the wall clock: find it in the service you sat through 0:13:07 — the offset: find it in the recording; same number the .srt uses

And it cannot take a service down. Lines are batched every five seconds, a hiccup retries with the words still buffered, and a permanent failure stops the Doc for the rest of the service and says so on the Captions tab — while the screen, YouTube and the .srt carry on.

Like you're five

Four friends listen to the robot. One holds up the sign. One phones YouTube. One writes a subtitle file for the recording. And one keeps a diary in nice paragraphs — and if the diary-writer's pen breaks, the other three don't even look up.

The operator's view

The little website

npm run dev starts a small web app on the LAN and prints one address per network. It starts on a machine with nothing configured — no API key, no database — and lists what's missing with one thing to do about each, instead of refusing to run. Closing the page stops nothing: the bridge lives in the terminal, and only Ctrl+C ends it.

Being on the network is the login

Every page carries a ?token= because a vMix Browser input cannot type a password. The homepage hands that token to any browser asking from a private address — that's what makes the short URL work. So the mandir LAN is the boundary: anyone in the building can open the app; nobody outside can. Which is exactly why the port must never be forwarded to the internet.

The sixteen-doorbell problem

A capture card is one device with many inputs — a Blackmagic card presents sixteen, and the speaker is on one of them. Mixing all sixteen together averages one voice with fifteen silent channels: 24 dB down, a meter that barely moves, and a transcription of nothing — with no error anywhere, because nothing is technically wrong. Test this input listens for four seconds, shows the level on every channel separately, and picks the live one for you:

Input 1−27.3 dB Input 2silent Input 3silent ⋮  inputs 4–16 — all silent

Sound on input 1 only, out of 16 → chosen for you. Sixteen silent channels means the device is fine and nothing is reaching it.

Like you're five

Being inside the house is the ticket — anyone in the house can use the remote, and the front door stays locked to the street. And the mixing desk is one doorbell with sixteen buttons: press all sixteen at once and one tiny ring gets drowned out by fifteen silences. The app presses each button and tells you which one actually rings.

Vocabulary

Teaching it temple words

A generic transcriber makes a mess of Swaminarayan vocabulary. So the bridge ships with a glossary — 202 translation terms, 86 listening terms, and a block of instructions setting the register: this is a sermon, not a podcast; grace and blessings, never a literal reading that lands somewhere awkward on a public broadcast. It was ported, with thanks, from the Aashirwad Captions bridge run by the American mandirs.

A glossary is a soft bias — Soniox paraphrases through it often enough that a second pass exists. src/soniox/normalize.ts corrects the English afterwards: Jai becomes Jay, lineage titles stay as names instead of turning into "the life-breath of our life", and દ્વિભુજ is rendered two-armed rather than bipedal.

Curation happens on the Glossary tab. Your terms override the built-ins; an overridden built-in shows what it replaced and can be restored; nothing is ever deleted. Changes apply the next time captions start — Soniox can't be re-taught mid-session.

Like you're five

You give the robot a list of your family's names and your special words so it doesn't guess. It still gets one wrong sometimes, so a proofreader with a red pen fixes the same known mistakes every time, the moment before the words go out.

Memory

Finding a moment again

search "what does devotion require" finds passages by meaning, not keywords, across every ingested sermon — and both languages are indexed, so an English question can find a Gujarati passage. Results link like sermon.mp4#t=1727: click and the video jumps to that second.

The understanding model runs on your machine — a one-off ~120 MB download, no API key, nothing sent anywhere. Sermons are indexed as 1–3 minute passages rather than single subtitles, because one subtitle is too small a thought to search for.

Like you're five

Ask "where did he talk about being kind?" and it flips through every notebook and opens the video at the right page — even if he never said the word "kind", and even if you asked in the other language.

Invariants

Rules the code never breaks

A few promises hold everywhere, because breaking them is expensive or cruel to undo. The full list, with reasoning, lives in SPEC.md.

  • The video file is never touched. Subtitles are separate files beside it. A correction never re-encodes anything.
  • Words shown are never changed. No provisional text, no re-ordering under the reader's eyes. Four separate mechanisms enforce it, from the wire protocol up.
  • Nothing flashes. No subtitle lives shorter than 1.5 seconds. This rule outranks every size limit.
  • Human corrections are never silently destroyed. Re-ingesting an edited sermon is refused; every edit keeps who, when, and the words before.
  • Secrets never go in config.json. That file is committed to git. Keys live in .env, which never is.
  • The database is the only source of truth. Files, YouTube tracks, the projector — all regenerated copies, all disposable.
